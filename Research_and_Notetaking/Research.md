# Research And Writing

## Learning

Learning a new skill, technology or concept takes primarily time and effort. This effort is materialized in the form of reading, watching a video,
listening to a podcast, attending a course, taking notes and practicing. All of which cost time. Learning always takes time, but there are some strategies
which can help to optimize the learning process.

### Strategies for Learning and Solving Problems Better

- **Active Learning**: Do not just listen or read passively. Practice, ask questions, try making hypotheses and test them.

- **Take Notes**: Either by hand or digitally, taking notes helps reinforce what you learn. Summarize key points in your own words.

- **Teach Others**: Explaining concepts to others forces you to clarify your understanding and identify gaps in your knowledge.

- **Make a Plan**: Set specific learning goals and break them down into manageable tasks. This helps maintain focus and track progress. Also, if the plan fails, then proceed to make
the necessary adjustments.

- **Practice Regularly**: Consistent practice is more effective than cramming. Prepare a set of practice material and work on it. Choose material that is relevant to your learning goals.

- **Seek Feedback**: Share your work with peers or mentors to get constructive criticism.

- **Exam Conditions**: Simulate exam conditions by timing yourself and working without resources. This helps build confidence and identify areas for improvement.

- **Revisit and Reflect**: Regularly review what you’ve learned.

- **Breaks**: Do not overdo it. Take breaks and do other activities to let your brain rest and process information.

- **Sleep**: Sleep is crucial for memory consolidation.

- **Use the Horizon**: When trying to solve a problem, do not just focus on the problem itself and think about solving it mechanically. Instead, look at the bigger picture,
consider the context, ask how does this problem relate to other problems, and how does it fit into the overall system or project, which techniques or tools can be used to solve it, etc.

## Planning

- **Find a topic**: Start from general to specific by narrowing the topic of interest. It is also important to
see the bigger picture and the connection to other related or maybe not so related areas; something interesting can come out of that.
Be careful to not pick a topic which has already been handled.

- **Find the initial sources**: find sources while also being critical about the trust of the source. This does not mean to only take papers, because
less serious sources can lead you to more serious references. Be extremely critical about the content.

- **Find the serious sources**: create a list/graph of connected ideas with their respective source. This time
only formal sources are allowed. This will help to understand the topic and already giving you the opportunity to organize the bibliography.
Be extremely critical about the content, this time like really: look for biases, fallacies, who paid for the paper, and finally find contrary papers if possible.

- **Make the writing plan**: Organize what is going to be in the individual sections of your paper with a deadline if possible.
Document everything, break down the plan into small goals and plan the time for doing: experiments, proofs, etc.

- **Miscellaneous**:
  - Make a pre-writing: drafts.
  - Do not delete what you have already written. Keep it with version control.

## Structure

Template structure of all academic papers

- **Title**: Short descriptive sentence about the paper. Include keywords if possible and do not lie.

- **Abstract**: Concise summary with the main selling points of the paper. It should be descriptive but non-technical for others to understand. A good guide is the
**What/Why/How** structure.

- **Introduction**: Normally start with a longer summary with more background, context, methods, results, etc. References can be included. It is also a good start to
introduce notation and jargon. Finally, adding information about the structure of the paper and the way of understanding your thesis.

- **Contributions**: What are the results.

- **Overview**: A small explanation about the structure of the paper.

- **Body**: Main section of the paper including the experiments, proofs, analysis. It has to be structured into individual sections dedicated to
each of the topics under a logical and concise structure.

- **Discussion**: This is part of the body, but it can be handled as its own manner. Here the results should be compared to other references, the meaning should be
investigated, maybe we are refuting another paper; thus, we want to provide a good argument about our results and the methods we used. It is also the place to put
our research into its place in the bigger picture.

- **Conclusion**: Summary again of the results in more detail than in the abstract. We can also propose other open questions derived from our research and make a sort of
teaser for future research.

- **Appendix**: Side details, introductory material, additional tables, code, graphs.

- **References**: Organized, consistent list of references used in the paper.

## Literature

### Primary Sources

Original sources about the topic written by the first person interested in the topic.

### Secondary Sources

Literature written from the primary sources.

### Where to find sources

- **Use Wikipedia**: Wikipedia is one of the best ways to make a first impression, find sources, related persons.
- **Google Scholar**: Easiest way.
- **The university library**
- **EBSCO**
- **Sections of science books**

#### Google Scholar Search

Use boolean operations like **AND**, **OR**, **-**, with **" "** for strings with spaces; inside the search-bar to get better results.

### Predatory Journals

Due to the economic structure our world has, there are cases where the results of a research procedure whose
results are worded as negative are often not directly published. Not because the research was bad, but because positive results
sell better.

There are journals which take advantage of this monetary situation and of the research of scientist which offer
easy publication under a pay-wall. The so-called **predatory journals** operate by:

- Not having a review procedure.
- Not being strict on their guidelines.
- Having to pay to publish instead of after published.

They are **not** suited for literature.

## Language

### General Guidelines for Papers

- Use the we-perspective.
- Do not mix your own opinion.
- No vague language, leave no room for interpretation even if that means repetition in the text.

### Punctuation Rules

#### Comma `,`

The comma separates elements within a sentence without creating a full stop.

- **Listing items**: Use a comma to separate three or more items in a series. The last comma before *and* or *or* is the **Oxford comma** and is recommended in academic writing.
  - *We analyzed the data, ran the simulations, and reviewed the results.*
  - *The model depends on temperature, pressure, and density.*

- **Joining independent clauses**: Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (**for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so** — FANBOYS) when it joins two independent clauses.
  - *The experiment was repeated three times, but the results remained inconsistent.*
  - *We increased the sample size, and the variance decreased significantly.*

- **Introductory elements**: Use a comma after an introductory word, phrase, or clause.
  - *However, the results did not confirm the hypothesis.*
  - *In this section, we describe the methodology.*
  - *After running the model, we observed a convergence.*

- **Non-restrictive clauses**: Use commas to set off extra information that is not essential to the meaning of the sentence (surrounded by commas, or at the end preceded by one).
  - *The algorithm, which was introduced in 2018, outperforms its predecessors.*
  - *Newton's second law, as described earlier, forms the basis of this model.*

- **Coordinate adjectives**: Use a comma between adjectives that independently modify the same noun (test: if *and* fits between them, use a comma).
  - *It is a long, complex proof.*

- **Do NOT use** a comma to separate a subject from its verb, or between two items only joined by *and*/*or*.
  - ✗ *The results, were unexpected.*
  - ✗ *We collected data, and analyzed it.*

---

#### Period `.`

The period marks the end of a declarative or imperative sentence.

- **End of sentence**: Every complete sentence ends with a period.
  - *The function converges to zero as n approaches infinity.*

- **Abbreviations**: Use a period in certain abbreviations. Note that many academic style guides omit periods in acronyms (e.g., *USA*, not *U.S.A.*).
  - *Fig., et al., i.e., e.g.*

- **Decimal numbers**: Use a period as the decimal separator in English (not a comma, as in many other languages).
  - *The error rate was 0.034.*

- **Do NOT use** a period after headings, table entries, or display equations.

---

#### Colon `:`

The colon introduces something that follows logically from the preceding clause. The clause before the colon must always be a complete sentence.

- **Introducing a list**: Use a colon after a complete sentence to introduce a list.
  - *The method consists of three steps: initialization, iteration, and convergence.*
  - ✗ *The steps are: initialization, iteration, and convergence.* (the clause before is incomplete)

- **Introducing a quotation or explanation**: Use a colon to introduce a direct quote or an elaboration.
  - *The conclusion is straightforward: the model fails under high noise conditions.*
  - *Shannon stated the following: "Information is the resolution of uncertainty."*

- **Between independent clauses**: Use a colon when the second clause explains or illustrates the first.
  - *The result was unexpected: the accuracy dropped by 40% when the dataset was doubled.*

- **After a colon**, the first word is generally lowercase unless it introduces a proper noun or begins a complete quoted sentence.

---

#### Semicolon `;`

The semicolon is stronger than a comma but weaker than a period. It connects closely related ideas at the same level of importance.

- **Joining independent clauses**: Use a semicolon to join two independent clauses that are closely related, without a conjunction.
  - *The first model was simple; the second incorporated non-linear terms.*
  - *Data collection ended in March; analysis began the following month.*

- **Before conjunctive adverbs**: Use a semicolon before words like *however, therefore, moreover, furthermore, consequently, thus, otherwise* when they connect two independent clauses, and a comma after them.
  - *The sample size was small; however, the results were statistically significant.*
  - *The proof was incomplete; therefore, we revised the assumptions.*

- **Separating complex list items**: Use a semicolon to separate items in a list when the items themselves contain commas.
  - *The study covered Berlin, Germany; Paris, France; and Rome, Italy.*
  - *We compared three models: the baseline, which was linear; the improved model, which was quadratic; and the final model, which was non-linear.*

#### Dash

There are two kinds of dashes: the **en dash** (–) and the **em dash** (—).

- **Em dash** `—`: Used to mark a strong interruption, elaboration, or aside — more emphatic than a comma or parenthesis.
  - *The assumption — which had never been challenged — turned out to be wrong.*
  - *We found the opposite result — a significant increase in entropy.*

- **En dash** `–`: Used for ranges (numbers, dates, pages) and to connect compound adjectives where one element is a multi-word expression.
  - *See pages 14–27.*
  - *The 2020–2025 dataset was used.*
  - *A post–Cold War analysis.*

- Do not use a hyphen `-` as a substitute for a dash in formal writing.

#### Hyphen `-`

- Used to form compound modifiers before a noun.
  - *A well-known result, a long-term solution, a data-driven approach.*
- Used with prefixes before proper nouns or to avoid ambiguity.
  - *non-Euclidean, pre-2020, re-evaluate.*
- **Do NOT hyphenate** compound modifiers that come after the noun.
  - *The approach is data driven.* (no hyphen after the noun)

#### Parentheses `( )`

- Used to insert supplementary or clarifying information that could be removed without changing the meaning.
  - *The model (described in Section 3) achieved the best performance.*
  - *The result was statistically significant (p < 0.05).*
- If the parenthetical is a complete sentence, the period goes inside. Otherwise, it goes outside.
  - *See the appendix for details. (A detailed proof is provided there.)*
  - *The values are included in the table (see Table 2).*

#### Quotation Marks `" "`

- Used for direct quotations and to introduce a term being defined or used in a special sense.
  - *Turing defined "intelligence" operationally through his imitation game.*
- In academic writing, use double quotation marks. Single marks are for quotations within quotations.
- Periods and commas go inside the quotation marks in American English; outside in British English. Be consistent.

#### Ellipsis `…`

- Used to indicate an omission within a quoted passage.
  - *"We propose… a framework based on iterative refinement."*
- Do not use ellipses to trail off in academic writing.

### English Tenses

| Tense | Name | Use Case | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple Present | Present Simple | General truths, habits, facts | *The model converges after ten iterations.* |
| Present Continuous | Present Progressive | Actions happening now or temporary situations | *We are currently analyzing the data.* |
| Present Perfect | Present Perfect | Past actions with present relevance; results of past actions | *Several studies have confirmed this result.* |
| Present Perfect Cont. | Present Perfect Progressive | Ongoing actions that started in the past | *Researchers have been developing this method since 2015.* |
| Simple Past | Past Simple | Completed actions at a specific past time | *We collected the samples in January.* |
| Past Continuous | Past Progressive | Actions ongoing at a specific past moment | *The algorithm was running when the error occurred.* |
| Past Perfect | Past Perfect | Action completed before another past action | *The model had already been trained before the test began.* |
| Past Perfect Cont. | Past Perfect Progressive | Ongoing past action before another past event | *We had been refining the approach for months before publishing.* |
| Simple Future | Future Simple | Predictions, decisions, promises | *Future work will address this limitation.* |
| Future Continuous | Future Progressive | Actions that will be ongoing at a future moment | *At that point, the system will be processing live data.* |
| Future Perfect | Future Perfect | Actions completed before a specific future time | *By next year, we will have validated the full dataset.* |
| Future Perfect Cont. | Future Perfect Progressive | Ongoing actions up to a future point | *By the time of submission, we will have been testing for six months.* |

**Note for academic writing**: Use the **simple present** for stating known results and facts, the **simple past** for describing what was done in the study, and the **present perfect** for referring to prior literature.
